## **Why Endocrinologists Care About It**

Vitamin D is far more than a dietary supplement. It is a **steroid hormone essential for calcium balance, skeletal integrity, immune regulation, and multiple metabolic processes.** In endocrinology, vitamin D status is one of the most common and clinically important laboratory concerns.

Geography plays a major role in vitamin D sufficiency. In regions above approximately 37° north latitude, such as the Pacific Northwest, ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation during fall and winter months is insufficient for adequate skin production of vitamin D. Even individuals who spend time outdoors may not generate enough vitamin D, particularly with appropriate and recommended sunscreen use.

Dietary sources are limited. Only a few foods naturally contain meaningful amounts of vitamin D (such as fatty fish and egg yolks), and fortified foods contribute variably.

According to the 2017–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), approximately **21% of the U.S. population had suboptimal vitamin D levels**,  highlighting how common this issue remains.

---

## **Vitamin D Chemistry and Activation**

Vitamin D synthesis begins in the skin following exposure to UVB radiation (not UVA, and not reliably from tanning beds). The molecule then undergoes two critical activation steps:

1. **Liver:** Conversion to 25-hydroxyvitamin D \[25(OH)D\]
2. **Kidneys:** Conversion to the active form, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D \[1,25(OH)₂D\]

This process depends on:

* Healthy skin function
* Normal liver activity
* Adequate kidney function
* Proper regulation by parathyroid hormone (PTH)

Because vitamin D functions as a hormone, its production and activation are tightly regulated by the endocrine system.

---

## **Why We Measure 25(OH)D — Not the Active Form**

A common misconception is that the active hormone, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, should be measured to assess vitamin D status. From an endocrinology perspective, this is usually incorrect.

We measure **25(OH)D** because:

* It is the **major circulating form** (the body's "storage" form).
* It has a **long half-life (2–3 weeks)**.
* It reflects total vitamin D input from sun exposure and diet.

In contrast, 1,25(OH)₂D:

* Has a very short half-life (4–6 hours).
* Is tightly regulated by PTH.
* May remain normal or even elevated in deficiency.

There are specific clinical scenarios, such as advanced kidney disease or hypoparathyroidism, where measuring 1,25(OH)₂D may be appropriate, but it is not a routine test for assessing vitamin D stores.

## **Interpreting Vitamin D Levels**

Serum 25(OH)D is considered the gold standard test.

General reference ranges:

* **Sufficiency:** ≥30 ng/mL (≥75 nmol/L)
* **Insufficiency:** 20–29 ng/mL
* **Deficiency:** \<20 ng/mL

(Reference ranges may vary slightly depending on laboratory standards and clinical guidelines.)

## **Physiological Roles of Vitamin D**

### **1\. Calcium and Bone Metabolism (Classic Role)**

Vitamin D increases intestinal calcium and phosphate absorption.

When levels are low:

* Calcium absorption decreases.
* PTH increases.
* Calcium is mobilized from bone.

Chronic deficiency can lead to:

* Rickets (in children)
* Osteomalacia (in adults)
* Osteoporosis
* Increased fracture risk

Vitamin D deficiency is one of the most modifiable risk factors for metabolic bone disease.

---

### **2\. Muscle Strength and Fall Prevention**

Adequate vitamin D levels support:

* Muscle strength
* Neuromuscular coordination
* Reduced fall risk in older adults

Even mild deficiency may contribute to muscle weakness and instability.

---

### **3\. Metabolic Regulation**

Vitamin D receptors are present in pancreatic beta cells and adipose tissue. Research suggests vitamin D may influence:

* Insulin secretion
* Insulin sensitivity
* Weight regulation

While associations exist, supplementation beyond correcting deficiency has not consistently shown dramatic metabolic benefits in clinical trials.

---

### **4\. Immune System Modulation**

Vitamin D plays an important role in immune balance:

* Enhances antimicrobial peptide production
* Modulates inflammatory cytokine responses
* Supports immune tolerance

Low levels have been associated with certain autoimmune and inflammatory conditions, though supplementation outcomes vary depending on the condition.

---

### **5\. Cellular Growth and Cancer Research**

Vitamin D influences:

* Cell proliferation
* Differentiation
* Apoptosis

Low vitamin D levels are associated with certain malignancies; however, large clinical trials have not consistently demonstrated that supplementation prevents cancer in the general population.

---

## **Who Should Be Screened?**

Screening should be individualized based on risk factors rather than performed universally in low-risk individuals.

Higher-risk populations include:

* Older adults
* Postmenopausal women
* Patients with osteoporosis
* Chronic kidney disease
* Hyperparathyroidism
* Obesity
* Malabsorption syndromes
* Post-bariatric surgery patients
* Certain autoimmune conditions

Vitamin D deficiency is often silent and discovered incidentally during laboratory evaluation.

---

## **Supplementation: Evidence-Based Approach**

Vitamin D supplementation must be individualized. More is not better.

Excessive supplementation can lead to toxicity, resulting in:

* Hypercalcemia
* Kidney stones
* Kidney injury
* Vascular calcification

### **Typical Dosing**

* **Maintenance dose for adults:** 800–2,000 IU daily
* Dose adjustments are based on measured 25(OH)D levels
* Severe deficiency may require high-dose weekly therapy for 8–12 weeks, followed by maintenance dosing
* Patients with malabsorption or intestinal disorders may require higher daily doses

### **Vitamin D2 vs D3**

* **Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol)** is generally preferred
* It raises serum 25(OH)D more efficiently
* It has a longer duration of action

Vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) remains an acceptable alternative in certain cases, particularly in prescription formulations.

---

## **Key Takeaways**

* Vitamin D is a hormone, not simply a supplement.
* Deficiency is common, especially in higher latitudes.
* 25(OH)D is the correct test for assessing status.
* Bone health remains its most well-established role.
* When looking for a supplement, always verify the label for serving size. 25 mcg of D3 \= 1000 IU (international units).
* Supplementation should be personalized and monitored.

In endocrinology, optimizing vitamin D status represents one of the most accessible and modifiable interventions to support long-term skeletal and metabolic health.
